Lesson 3.02
★ One Vowel Change — The Present Subjunctive
Lesson · about 40 minutes
📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
Yesterday you met the mood. Today you build it — and it costs you exactly one vowel.
The present active subjunctive is formed by altering the characteristic vowel of each conjugation and then adding the ordinary active personal endings. One mnemonic covers all four families. Learn it here, and every present subjunctive you ever meet is just this pattern applied to a new stem.

📚 PART 2 · THE VOWEL RULE 12 min

Change the vowel, add the endings

The present active subjunctive is formed by altering the characteristic vowel of each conjugation, then adding the normal active personal endings -m, -s, -t, -mus, -tis, -nt (note: 1st singular is -m, not -ō).

💡 THE MNEMONIC

Remember “She eats A BIG elephant.” Each vowel of the phrase is the subjunctive vowel for a conjugation, in order.

ConjugationVowel shiftSignal vowel
1st-ā- → -ē-amēs
2nd-ē- → -eā-moneās
3rdshort -e- → -ā-dūcās
3rd-iō & 4thadd -iā-capiās, audiās

A single sentence captures the whole rule: the first conjugation swaps its ā for ē; every other conjugation brings in an ā.

📌 PART 3 · TWO FULL PARADIGMS 10 min

The pattern in full

Here are the model verbs amō (1st) and dūcō (3rd), fully conjugated in the present active subjunctive. English glosses use “may” because the subjunctive alone carries no single fixed translation — its meaning comes from the clause it sits in.

amō — 1st conjugation

amem — I may love
amēs — you may love
amet — he/she may love
amēmus — we may love
amētis — you all may love
ament — they may love

dūcō — 3rd conjugation

dūcam — I may lead
dūcās — you may lead
dūcat — he/she may lead
dūcāmus — we may lead
dūcātis — you all may lead
dūcant — they may lead
⚠️ CAUTION · FUTURE vs. SUBJUNCTIVE

The 3rd conjugation present subjunctive can look identical to a 1st/2nd conjugation future indicative in some forms (compare dūcat, subjunctive, with amābit, future). Your guides are the same three every time: context, connecting words (ut, nē, cum), and the verb's own conjugation. Never read a form in isolation — read the clause it belongs to.

Out loud: how would you tell dūcat (subjunctive) apart from amābit (future) in a real sentence? Commit to a rule, then reveal.

Three checks, together: (1) the verb's conjugation — dūcō is 3rd, so an -ā- form is subjunctive, while a 1st/2nd conj. verb spells its future with -bi-; (2) connecting words — ut, nē, cum almost always introduce a subjunctive; (3) context — a subordinate clause of purpose or result wants the subjunctive. Read the whole clause, never the bare form.
