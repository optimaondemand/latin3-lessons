⏰ PART 1 · THE IMPERFECT SUBJUNCTIVE 9 min

Infinitive + endings, no exceptions

The imperfect active subjunctive is the simplest tense to form in the language: take the present active infinitive and add the normal active personal endings -m, -s, -t, -mus, -tis, -nt. Every conjugation follows this rule without exception.

💡 THE RULE IN ONE LINE

amāre + -m, -s, -t, -mus, -tis, -nt → amārem, amārēs, amāret, amārēmus, amārētis, amārent. For 3rd conjugation: dūcere → dūcerem, dūcerēs… The long -ē- right before the ending is your signal.

amō — imperfect subjunctive

amārem — I might love
amārēs — you might love
amāret — he/she might love
amārēmus — we might love
amārētis — you all might love
amārent — they might love

dūcō — imperfect subjunctive

dūcerem — I might lead
dūcerēs — you might lead
dūceret — he/she might lead
dūcerēmus — we might lead
dūcerētis — you all might lead
dūcerent — they might lead

A useful shortcut for reading: if a form looks like the whole infinitive with a personal ending stuck on the end, it is an imperfect subjunctive.

📚 PART 2 · SEQUENCE OF TENSES 10 min

The main verb governs the subordinate one

When a subjunctive verb appears in a subordinate clause, its tense is governed by the tense of the main verb — a principle called sequence of tenses. This module introduces the basic rule; purpose and result clauses in the next module will apply it in full.

Primary sequence

Main verb: present / future / future perfect
Sub. clause: present subjunctive (same time) or perfect subjunctive (before).

Rogat quid faciant.
“He asks what they are doing.”

Secondary sequence

Main verb: imperfect / perfect / pluperfect
Sub. clause: imperfect subjunctive (same time) or pluperfect subjunctive (before).

Rogāvit quid facerent.
“He asked what they were doing.”

At this stage, focus on recognizing the pattern and identifying whether a subjunctive form is present or imperfect. Full application comes in Module 04 with purpose and result clauses.

Commit, then reveal: how do you form the imperfect subjunctive of any verb, and how does that make nōlle (the irregular infinitive of nōlō) easy?

Take the present infinitive and add the active endings. Because the rule uses the infinitive itself, even irregular verbs are trivial: nōlle + endings → nōllem, nōllēs, nōllet, nōllēmus, nōllētis, nōllent (“I might be unwilling…”). No stem-hunting required — the infinitive already is the base.
