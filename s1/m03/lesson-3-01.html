★ A Third Mood — From Fact to Possibility
Lesson · about 35 minutes
📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
Until now, your verbs have stated facts. This module gives you the mood Latin uses for everything that is not simply a fact.
Every verb you have conjugated so far has been indicative — it indicates, it states what is. You have also used the imperative to give commands. This module opens the third and richest mood: the subjunctive. It is the mood of contingency, purpose, result, doubt, wish, and reported thought — the grammar of what might, should, or would happen. This week you learn to form and recognize it in the present and imperfect; over the next modules you will learn every use it drives. There is no drill today — just the big idea and the story we will read it in.

⏰ PART 1 · WARM-UP 5 min

Mood is attitude toward reality

In grammar, mood is not emotion — it is the speaker's stance toward the reality of an action. English marks this too, though quietly. Compare these two sentences and listen for the difference.

She is here.

Indicative — a plain statement of fact.

I wish she were here.

Subjunctive — not a fact, but a wish; note the odd “were,” not “was.”

💭 QUICK CHECK-IN · no wrong answer
English keeps only a few subjunctive scraps (“if I were you,” “God save the Queen”). What do those phrases have in common?
📚 PART 2 · THE BIG IDEA 12 min

Indicative vs. subjunctive

The subjunctive is a mood, not a tense. It signals contingency, purpose, result, doubt, or reported thought — situations where the action is not simply stated as a fact. Where the indicative reports, the subjunctive projects.

Indicative — fact

Servus fugit.
The slave is fleeing.

States what is actually happening.

Subjunctive — contingency

…ut servus fugiat.
…so that the slave may flee.

Projects a purpose — not (yet) a fact.

Latin's subjunctive drives a whole family of constructions you will meet across this course: purpose and result clauses, indirect questions, cum-clauses, conditions, wishes, and more. Before you can use any of them, you must be able to build the subjunctive and tell its tense apart from the indicative. That is exactly this module's job: the present and imperfect subjunctive, plus a first look at how a main verb governs the tense of a subordinate one.

💡 WHY THIS IS THE HINGE OF LATIN 3

Roughly half of everything an author like Cicero, Caesar, or Plautus writes in subordinate clauses is subjunctive. Master its formation now, in two clean tenses, and the sophisticated syntax of the rest of the year becomes recognition rather than guesswork.

In your own words: what is the difference between mood and tense? Commit to an answer out loud, then reveal.

Tense tells you when an action happens (present, imperfect, perfect…). Mood tells you the speaker's attitude toward the action's reality — whether it is asserted as fact (indicative), commanded (imperative), or projected as possible/purposed/wished (subjunctive). A verb carries both at once: e.g., the present subjunctive is present in tense, subjunctive in mood.
🎭 THIS WEEK'S TEXT 3 min
🏛️ PLAUTUS · MĪLES GLŌRIŌSUS, ACTS III–IV

Titus Maccius Plautus (c. 254–184 BC) wrote the earliest complete Latin literature we still have: rowdy, fast comedies for the Roman stage. Mīles Glōriōsus — “The Braggart Soldier” — gives us the archetype of the vain warrior, all boast and no sense, who is outwitted by a clever slave.

On Day 4 you will read an adapted passage in which the resourceful woman Philocōmasia pretends to be her own twin sister to fool her guard. It is a scene of pretense, plans, and things that are made to seem true — the perfect home for the mood of the not-quite-real.
