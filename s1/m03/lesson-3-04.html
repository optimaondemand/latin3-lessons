Lesson 3.04
★ Reading Plautus — A Woman, a Window, and a Twin Who Isn't There
Lesson · about 40 minutes
📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
Formation plus vocabulary equals reading. Today the subjunctives you built come alive inside a real Roman comedy.
No new grammar today. You read an adapted passage from Plautus's Mīles Glōriōsus, in which the clever Philocomasia pretends to be her own twin to fool the slave who guards her. Watch for the present and imperfect subjunctives from this week — and for a masterclass in dramatic irony, where the audience knows everything the guard does not.

⏰ PART 1 · THE TEXT 15 min

Philocōmasia Germānam Simulat

Adapted from Plautus, Mīles Glōriōsus, Acts III–IV. Read it aloud once for sound, then again slowly for sense, using the gloss beneath.

1.Palaestrio Philocōmasiam hortātus est ut germānam suam simulāret. 2.Philocōmasia, mulier ingeniōsa, intellēxit quid facere dēbēret. 3.Scelēdrus, servus custōs, per fenestram vidit nē quid malī accideret. 4.Sed ecce! Simul vīdit Philocōmasiam in alterā domō et hanc quam custōdiēbat. 5.“Quid hoc sit,” rogāvit, “nescio. Nōnne haec eadem mulier est?” 6.Palaestrio rīdēbat et tandem locūtus est: “Cōnsilium meum prōcedit.” 7.Philocōmasia inde ad mīlitem rediit et simulāvit sē numquam inde abiisse. 8.Mīles ipse, glōriā suā captus, nihil intellēxit et omnēs eius mendāciīs crēdidērunt.
Gloss: germānam — twin sister (acc.) · mulier ingeniōsa — a clever/resourceful woman · fenestram — window (acc.) · ecce — look! behold! · alterā domō — the other house (abl.) · custōdiēbat — he was guarding · rīdēbat — was laughing · prōcedit — is going well, proceeding · mendāciīs — lies (dat.); crēdidērunt — they believed (+dat.) · Note: ut simulāret (line 1) and nē accideret (line 3) are purpose clauses — formally introduced in Module 04.
📚 PART 2 · LITERARY LENS 8 min
🎭 DRAMATIC IRONY

Lines 3–5 are a masterpiece of dramatic irony: Scelēdrus sees what he thinks is an impossibility — the same woman in two places — but the audience knows exactly what is happening. Plautus's staging puts the audience in Palaestrio's position: omniscient, amused, superior to every character who does not know the plan. The subjunctive in line 3 (nē quid malī accideret) is itself ironic: Scelēdrus is guarding against harm — but harm to the scheme is already impossible, because he has been outmaneuvered before the scene even begins.

🔎 SUBJUNCTIVES IN THE WILD

Track the mood as you reread: simulāret (l.1) and accideret (l.3) are imperfect subjunctives (secondary sequence, after perfect/past main verbs); dēbēret (l.2) is imperfect subjunctive in an indirect question; sit (l.5) is a present subjunctive in an indirect question after the present-tense frame of Scelēdrus's speech. Every one of them is a form you built this week.

Commit, then reveal — translate three lines. Write your own English first; then check it against the model. Notice the syntax notes.

1. Palaestrio Philocōmasiam hortātus est ut germānam suam simulāret.

Palaestrio urged Philocomasia to pretend to be her twin sister. — ut + simulāret: purpose clause (Module 04); hortātus est: deponent perfect of hortor.

2. Scelēdrus per fenestram vidit nē quid malī accideret.

Sceledrus watched through the window so that nothing bad would happen (lest anything bad occur). — nē + accideret: negative purpose clause; imperfect subjunctive in secondary sequence after vidit.

3. Mīles ipse, glōriā suā captus, nihil intellēxit.

The soldier himself, captured by his own glory, understood nothing. — glōriā suā captus: ablative absolute (perfect passive participle + noun); nihil intellēxit: indicative main clause — no subjunctive here.
