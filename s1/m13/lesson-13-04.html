📚 PART 1 · HISTORICAL SETTING 7 min
🏛️ THE CATILINARIAN CONSPIRACY · ROME, 63 BC

In 63 BC the consul Marcus Tullius Cicero uncovered a plot by the bankrupt aristocrat Lucius Sergius Catilina (Catiline) to seize power by force, cancel debts, and murder magistrates. Having failed to win the consulship legitimately, Catiline turned to armed conspiracy (coniūrātiō).

Cicero, forewarned, denounced Catiline to his face in the senate in a series of speeches — the Catilinarian Orations — whose opening still rings: Quō usque tandem abūtēre, Catilīna, patientiā nostrā? (“How long, Catiline, will you go on abusing our patience?”). Catiline fled the city; his fellow conspirators were arrested and executed. Cicero styled himself the savior of the republic (pāter patriae).

The episode became the model of the crisis-and-eloquence narrative in Roman letters — and, because Cicero's prose is built from exactly the subordinate clauses you have reviewed, it is the perfect ground on which to test the whole subjunctive system.

⏰ PART 2 · THE SYNTHESIS PASSAGE 14 min

All S1 constructions in Ciceronian context

Read each line aloud. For each, locate the subordinate clause(s), name the construction, and translate. Then use the gloss below to check your vocabulary.

1.Cicerō scrīpsit ut posterī intellegerent quanta virtūs in rē pūblicā Rōmānā esset.

2.Cum Catilīna coniūrātiōnem fēcisset, Cicerō ita dīligēns erat ut statim eum expelleret.

3.Rogābāmus num senātus omnia scīret et quid factūrus esset.

4.Imperāvit lēgātīs ut Catilīnam ex urbe pellerent nē rem pūblicam laederet.

5.Sī Catilīna Rōmae mānsit, omnēs cīvēs in perīculō fuērunt — hoc Cicerō vidēbat.

6.Nisi Cicerō cōnsul fuisset, nēmō esset quī tanta celeritāte rem pūblicam servāre posset.

7.Lēgātōs mīsit quī cum aliīs cīvitātibus loquerentur nē Catilīna auxilia acciperet.

8.Cum Catilīna exiisset, Cicerō tandem ōrātiōnem habuit quā glōriam aeternam meruit.

Gloss: posterī — posterity, future generations · Catilīna — Catiline (Roman senator who led a conspiracy in 63 BC) · coniūrātiōnem — conspiracy · dīligēns — diligent, careful · expelleret — might expel (impf. subj.) · factūrus esset — was going to do (fut. act. participle + esset = future-in-the-past) · pellerent — might drive out · laederet — might harm · celeritāte — with speed, swiftness · loquerentur — might speak · auxilia acciperet — might receive reinforcements · exiisset — had gone out (plupf. subj. of exeō) · quā — by which (abl. of the relative, instrument) · meruit — earned, deserved (perfect of mereō)
📝 PART 3 · COMMIT, THEN REVEAL 10 min

Three sentences: identify, then translate

For each, write down the construction(s) and a full translation before you reveal. This is the exact skill the graded work demands.

1. Cum Catilīna coniūrātiōnem fēcisset, Cicerō ita dīligēns erat ut statim eum expelleret.
Constructions: cum + plupf. subj. (fēcisset) = cum historical/circumstantial; ita…ut + impf. subj. (expelleret) = result clause. Translation: “When Catiline had made a conspiracy, Cicero was so diligent that he immediately expelled him.”
2. Nisi Cicerō cōnsul fuisset, nēmō esset quī tanta celeritāte rem pūblicam servāre posset.
Constructions: nisi + plupf. subj. (fuisset) / impf. subj. (esset) = contrary-to-fact (mixed: pluperfect CTF protasis, present CTF apodosis); nēmō esset quī…posset = relative clause of characteristic. Translation: “If Cicero had not been consul, there would be no one who could save the republic with such speed.”
3. Lēgātōs mīsit quī cum aliīs cīvitātibus loquerentur nē Catilīna auxilia acciperet.
Constructions: mīsit + quī + impf. subj. (loquerentur) = relative clause of purpose; nē + impf. subj. (acciperet) = negative purpose clause (nested inside the purpose relative). Translation: “He sent ambassadors to speak with other states so that Catiline would not receive reinforcements.”
