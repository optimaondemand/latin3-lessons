⏰ PART 1 · THE PASSAGE 14 min

Read for the grammar you just reviewed

Read the passage aloud. As you go, mark: two passive forms, one PPP used as a modifier, and the indirect statement. Verify your finds against the gloss below.

1. Plautus, poēta Rōmānus clārissimus, circā ducentōs annōs ante Christum nātum fābulās composuit. 2. Fābulae eius ā populō Rōmānō magnopere amātae sunt et in theātrīs multīs āctātae sunt. 3. Dīcitur Plautum vīgintī et ūnam fābulās Latīnās scrīpsisse. 4. In fābulīs eius servī callidī et mīlitēs arrogantēs semper reperiuntur. 5. Nōs hōc semestrī fābulam cuī nōmen est Mīles Glōriōsus legēmus et multa nova discēmus.
Gloss: clārissimus — most famous · circā + acc. — around, approximately · ante Christum nātum — before Christ was born (= BCE) · composuit — composed, wrote · āctātae sunt — were performed · dīcitur — it is said · reperiuntur — are found, are encountered · cuī nōmen est — whose name is · legēmus — we will read · discēmus — we will learn

Grammar to find: Passive forms: amātae sunt, āctātae sunt, dīcitur, reperiuntur · PPP as modifier: nātum (modifying Christum); amātae / āctātae in the passive perfects · Indirect statement: dīcitur Plautum … scrīpsisse (acc. subject Plautum + perfect infinitive scrīpsisse).

📚 PART 2 · PLAUTUS & ROMAN COMEDY 12 min
🎭 PLAUTUS AND THE FABULA PALLIĀTA

Titus Maccius Plautus (c. 254–184 BCE) wrote for a popular Roman audience. Roman comedy (fābula palliāta) adapted Greek New Comedy plots: clever slaves who outwit pompous masters, mercenary soldiers in love, parasites hunting for a free meal.

Plautus excels at rapid dialogue, wordplay, and social inversion. His Latin is colloquial and energetic — a very different register from Cicero's formal prose, which you will encounter in M10. Learning to navigate both registers is one of the goals of Latin 3 Honors.

🎪 WHY START WITH COMEDY?

Comedy rewards the reader who can hear tone. Mīles Glōriōsus — “The Braggart Soldier” — turns on the boasting of Pyrgopolīnicēs and the scheming of the slave Palaestrio. Reading it, you meet deponent verbs, the subjunctive, and purpose clauses in action, spoken by characters — not isolated in a chart.

✅ PART 3 · CHECK YOUR READING 14 min

Five checks — some on the Latin, some on the culture. Work slowly; the point is to prove you can read the passage and place Plautus. Wrong answers give a hint; use Reset to try again.

✅ TRY IT · find the passive
Which word from the passage is a passive form?
✅ TRY IT · the indirect statement

✅ TRY IT · translate
Translate line 2: Fābulae eius ā populō Rōmānō magnopere amātae sunt.
✅ TRY IT · the culture
Roman comedy (fābula palliāta) is best described as…
✅ TRY IT · register
Compared with Cicero, Plautus's Latin is…

Commit, then reveal — two sentences from the passage. Type or say your translation of each before you reveal. Watch the passive and the indirect statement.

2. Fābulae eius ā populō Rōmānō magnopere amātae sunt et in theātrīs multīs āctātae sunt.
His plays were greatly loved by the Roman people and were performed in many theaters. — two perfect passives (amātae sunt, āctātae sunt), each a PPP (fem. pl.) + sunt; ā populō is the personal agent.
3. Dīcitur Plautum vīgintī et ūnam fābulās Latīnās scrīpsisse.
It is said that Plautus wrote twenty-one Latin plays. — indirect statement after dīcitur: accusative subject Plautum + perfect infinitive scrīpsisse (action completed before “is said”).
