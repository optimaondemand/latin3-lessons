⏰ PART 1 · THE THREE PARTICIPLES 10 min

Verbal adjectives with a built-in clock

Latin has three common participles. Each carries the force of a verb and agrees with a noun in gender, number, and case. The key to translating them is time relative to the main verb.

ParticipleForm (amō)Voice & timeTranslation pattern
PAP — Present Activeamāns, amantisActive · simultaneousloving, while loving
PPP — Perfect Passiveamātus, -a, -umPassive · completed beforehaving been loved, loved
FAP — Future Activeamātūrus, -a, -umActive · future / intendedabout to love, intending to love

📚 PART 2 · THE ABLATIVE ABSOLUTE 8 min

A phrase that stands apart

📜 WHAT MAKES IT “ABSOLUTE”

A noun in the ablative + a participle in the ablative (agreeing with it) form an ablative absolute — a phrase grammatically independent of (“absolved” from) the main clause that expresses time, cause, or circumstance.

The noun in an ablative absolute cannot also be the subject of the main verb. That is the single most reliable test.

Mīlite captō, hostēs fūgērunt.

“With the soldier having been captured” / “After the soldier was captured, the enemies fled.” — mīlite (abl.) + captō (PPP, abl.); the soldier is not the subject of fūgērunt.

Translate it flexibly: “with X done,” “when/after/since X was done,” or a smooth English clause — whatever the context needs.

📌 PART 3 · INDIRECT STATEMENT + VOCABULARY 8 min

Accusative subject + infinitive

After verbs of saying, thinking, knowing, or perceiving, Latin uses an accusative subject + infinitive for indirect statement. The tense of the infinitive is relative to the main verb: present = same time, perfect = before, future = after.

InfinitiveTime relative to main verbExample
Present (-āre, -ēre…)SimultaneousDīcit Plautum fābulās amāre. — He says Plautus loves plays.
Perfect (-isse; PPP + esse)Completed beforeDīcit Plautum fābulās scrīpsisse. — He says Plautus wrote plays.
Future (FAP + esse)After the main verbDīcit eum plūrēs scrīptūrum esse. — He says he will write more.
📚 VOCABULARY · VERBA SENTIENDĪ ET DĪCENDĪ

These “verbs of perceiving and saying” introduce indirect statement — know them on sight: dīcō (say) · negō (say…not, deny) · putō (think) · crēdō (believe) · arbitror (judge) · sciō (know) · videō (see) · sentiō (perceive) · audiō (hear). Example: Palaestriō crēdit sē vincere — “Palaestrio believes that he is winning.”

✅ TRY IT · participle time
The PAP amāns describes an action that happens…

Commit, then reveal: explain why Fābulae ā Plautō scrīptae populārēs erant is not an ablative absolute, even though it contains a PPP. Say your reasoning, then reveal.

The PPP scrīptae is in the nominative, modifying the subject fābulae (“plays written by Plautus…”). An ablative absolute requires the noun and participle to both be in the ablative and to stand apart from the main clause. Here the participle is doing ordinary adjective work on the subject — so it is a nominative PPP, not an ablative absolute.
