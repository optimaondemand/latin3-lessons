⏰ PART 1 · LITERARY READING I 14 min

On Friendship — De Amicitia 20–22

Read the whole passage aloud first. Find each main verb, then the subordinate triggers, before you translate.

Dē Amīcitiā — Laelius speaks to Fannius and Mucius Scaevola (adapted)
1.Nihil est amīcitiā praestabilius, nihil tam aptum ad iūcunditātem vīvendī, nihil tam difficile quod amīcus nōn praestāre possit.
2.Amīcitia enim ex eā radīce nāscitur quae virtūs vocātur — sine virtūte amīcitia esse nōn potest.
3.Quī semel in amīcitiam recēperit eum, is dēbet eum tamquam alterum sē ipsum habēre.
4.Sunt quī putent ūtilitātem esse vinculum amīcitiae — at errō: nōn enim ūtilitās amīcitiam pepercit, sed amīcitia ūtilitātem.
5.Laelius recordābātur Scīpiōnis tamquam eius quī nusquam abfutūrus esset — ita vīvus in animō manēbat.
6.Nōbīs difficillimum est ut sine amīcō vīvāmus — at facilius sine multīs aliīs rēbus.
7.Omnia quae ad vītam beātam pertinent amīcitia continet — nēmō est quī sine eā beātus esse possit.
8.Haec tanta est vis amīcitiae ut, sī quis Scīpiōnem Laeliumque vīdisset, nōn intellegeret uter dominus, uter hospes esset.
Gloss: praestabilius — more excellent, more outstanding · iūcunditātem vīvendī — the pleasantness of living (gerund) · radīce — root (abl. of source) · semel — once, ever · recēperit — has received (perf. subj.; primary sequence) · alterum sē ipsum — a second self · vinculum — bond, chain · pepercit — here in a play on words, “produced” (the text turns an unusual usage) · abfutūrus esset — was going to be absent (fut. inf. in indirect statement, secondary sequence) · beātam — happy, blessed · pertinent — belong to, pertain to · uter — which of the two · hospes — guest-friend
📜 LITERARY CONTEXT · DE AMICITIA

Cicero wrote De Amicitia in 44 BC, shortly after the assassination of Caesar and the death of his beloved daughter Tullia. The dialogue is set fictitiously in 129 BC: the statesman Laelius speaks to his sons-in-law about his friendship with the recently dead Scipio Africanus the Younger. The dramatic irony — a man explaining friendship through the death of a friend, while Cicero himself is about to lose everything — is not accidental.

📚 PART 2 · LITERARY READING II 10 min

On Old Age — De Senectute 6–9

Dē Senectūte — Cato Major speaks of age and wisdom (adapted)
9.Nōn est enim senectūs obiicienda — nōn est illud tempus quod pertimēscendum sit.
10.Quī recte vīxerit ac bene mōrātus fuerit, is senectūtem nōn timet sed expectat.
11.Catō recordābātur quam multa studia, quam multī amīcī, quam magnae rēs gestae in vītā fuissent.
12.Illa quae multa et magna ēgimus in adulēscentiā — nōnne ad senectūtem memoriae iūcunditātem afferunt?
Gloss: obiicienda — to be objected to / cast against (gerundive, passive periphrastic) · pertimēscendum sit — ought to be greatly feared (gerundive + sit = subjunctive periphrastic; characteristic) · recte vīxerit — has lived rightly (perf. subj.; primary sequence) · bene mōrātus fuerit — has been of good character (perf. subj. of deponent mōror) · recordābātur — recalled (imperfect deponent) · fuissent — pluperf. subj. (indirect question, secondary sequence) · adulēscentiā — youth · memoriae iūcunditātem — the pleasantness to memory (dat. + acc.)
📜 LITERARY CONTEXT · DE SENECTUTE

Written in 44 BC alongside De Amicitia, De Senectute features the elder Cato (234–149 BC) as speaker. Cicero was 62 when he wrote it — in political exile from power after Caesar's death. The book is partly consolation, partly philosophical argument: that old age is not the deprivation of life's goods but their transformation. Reading it alongside De Amicitia, you see Cicero in conversation with death, loss, and the meaning of a life well lived.

✍️ PART 3 · COMMIT, THEN REVEAL 10 min

Three sentences from the reading

Write your own translation in the box before you reveal. This is the honest test of whether you read it or only recognized it.

1. Amīcitia enim ex eā radīce nāscitur quae virtūs vocātur — sine virtūte amīcitia esse nōn potest.
For friendship is born from that root which is called virtue — without virtue friendship cannot exist. quae virtūs vocātur = ordinary relative clause, indicative. nāscitur = deponent present. esse nōn potest = complementary infinitive.
2. Haec tanta est vis amīcitiae ut, sī quis Scīpiōnem Laeliumque vīdisset, nōn intellegeret uter dominus, uter hospes esset.
So great is the power of friendship that, if anyone had seen Scipio and Laelius, he would not have understood which was the host, which the guest. Tanta est…ut = result clause. sī…vīdisset…intellegeret = mixed contrary-to-fact condition (past protasis, present apodosis). uter…uter = indirect question nested inside the result clause.
3. Quī recte vīxerit ac bene mōrātus fuerit, is senectūtem nōn timet sed expectat.
Whoever has lived rightly and has been of good character, that person does not fear old age but awaits it. quī…vīxerit/fuerit = relative clause of characteristic (indefinite antecedent + perfect subjunctive). is = correlative pronoun referring back to quī.
✅ PART 4 · CHECK YOUR READING 6 min

Five checks — some test the syntax, some the sense of the passages. Wrong answers give a hint; use Reset to try again.

✅ TRY IT · the claim
In line 2, what does Cicero say friendship is born from?
✅ TRY IT · construction
In line 8, tanta est vis…ut…nōn intellegeret is what construction?
✅ TRY IT · the image
Line 3 says a true friend should be held as what?
✅ TRY IT · De Senectute
According to line 10, who does not fear old age?
✅ TRY IT · sequence
In line 11, recordābātur…fuissent, why is fuissent pluperfect subjunctive?
💭 QUICK CHECK-IN · no wrong answer
Which passage felt harder to read today?

Commit first: in one sentence, how do the two works speak to each other? Think it through, then reveal.

Both were written in 44 BC as Cicero faced Caesar's death, his daughter's death, and his own political end. De Amicitia argues that a life is made good by friendship rooted in virtue; De Senectute argues that such a life, well lived, makes even old age and the approach of death not something to fear but something whose memories bring pleasure. Together they are Cicero's consolation to himself.
