📚 PART 2 · THE READING STRATEGY 14 min

Five steps for an advanced period

Do not translate left-to-right. Latin word order is not English word order, and in Ciceronian prose the payoff often comes last. Work the sentence as a structure first.

📜 READING STRATEGY FOR ADVANCED CICERO

1. Read the whole sentence first, out loud, without translating.
2. Identify the main verb (an indicative usually marks the main clause).
3. Find every subordinate-clause trigger — ut, cum, sī, quī, quid…
4. Mark the mood of each subordinate verb and name the construction it belongs to.
5. Then translate outward from the main clause.

In long Ciceronian periods the main verb is often at or near the end of the sentence. Steps 1–2 keep you from committing to a translation before you have found the backbone; steps 3–4 map the branches; step 5 puts it into English only once you know the shape.

💡 WHY MOOD FIRST

At this level the mood of a subordinate verb is your fastest diagnostic. A subjunctive after ut could be purpose, result, or indirect command; an indicative in a relative clause is ordinary description, but a subjunctive there signals characteristic or purpose. Naming the construction before you translate keeps you from mistranslating the relationship between clauses.

🏛️ PART 3 · THE TWO WORKS 10 min

Cicero in conversation with death

📜 DE AMICITIA · ON FRIENDSHIP

Cicero wrote De Amicitia in 44 BC, shortly after the assassination of Caesar and the death of his beloved daughter Tullia. The dialogue is set fictitiously in 129 BC: the statesman Laelius speaks to his sons-in-law about his friendship with the recently dead Scipio Africanus the Younger. The dramatic irony — a man explaining friendship through the death of a friend, while Cicero himself is about to lose everything — is not accidental.

📜 DE SENECTUTE · ON OLD AGE

Written in 44 BC alongside De Amicitia, De Senectute features the elder Cato (234–149 BC) as speaker. Cicero was 62 when he wrote it — in political exile from power after Caesar's death. The book is partly consolation, partly philosophical argument: that old age is not the deprivation of life's goods but their transformation. Reading it alongside De Amicitia, you see Cicero in conversation with death, loss, and the meaning of a life well lived.

In your own words: why does naming a construction's mood come before translating it? Say your reasoning, then reveal.

Because mood tells you the relationship between clauses, not just the meaning of a word. A subjunctive after ut could be purpose, result, or command — each yields a different English sentence. If you fix the mood and construction first, you translate the logic correctly instead of guessing from vocabulary alone.
