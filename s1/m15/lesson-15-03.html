⏰ PART 1 · THE MODULE VOCABULARY 10 min

Module 15 words

Read each aloud with its principal parts and gender. These are the load-bearing nouns and verbs of the Catilinarian crisis.

coniūrātiō, -ōnis (f.)
conspiracy, plot
patria, -ae (f.)
fatherland, native country
exsilium, -ī (n.)
exile, banishment
senātus, -ūs (m.)
the Senate
cōnsul, cōnsulis (m.)
consul (Rome's chief magistrate)
audācia, -ae (f.)
audacity, reckless boldness
impūnītus, -a, -um
unpunished, without penalty
expellō, -ere (3rd)
to drive out, expel
📜 WORD ROOTS IN ENGLISH

Coniūrātiō (literally “a swearing-together”) gives conjure and conjuration; exsilium underlies exile; audācia gives audacious; expellō gives expel and expulsion. Latin's political vocabulary is still the vocabulary of politics.

📚 PART 2 · WORDS IN CONTEXT 8 min

Seeing the vocabulary at work

Before you drill, watch each key word do its job inside a Ciceronian phrase. Notice the grammatical case — that is often where the meaning lives.

Cōnstrictam iam omnium hōrum scientiā tenērī coniūrātiōnem tuam nōn vidēs?

“Do you not see that your conspiracy is already held bound by the knowledge of all these men?” — coniūrātiōnem acc. as subject of the infinitive tenērī (indirect statement).

Quod sī hoc…tempore patriā exire iussus essēs…

“But if at this time you had been ordered to leave your fatherland…” — patriā ablative of separation with exire.

…nōnne omnia istīus audāciae monumenta dēlēta essent?

“…would not all the monuments of that man's audacity have been destroyed?” — audāciae genitive, “of audacity.”

One natural rendering: Cōnsul coniūrātiōnem ā patriā expellit. — cōnsul nom. subject, coniūrātiōnem acc. object, ā patriā abl. of separation with expellit (3rd sg. pres.). The point of the exercise is the case logic, not one fixed word order.
