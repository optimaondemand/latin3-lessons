📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
Forms plus constructions equals reading. Today the week's grammar carries an argument — Cicero's own.
No new grammar today. ⏰ PART 1 · READ ALONG 14 min

Cicerō et Studium Philosophiae

Adapted from Cicero, De Officiis & Tusculanae Disputationes. Read each line aloud, find the gerund, and name its case and construction before you consult the gloss.

1.Cicerō ad Forum dīcendī causā cotīdiē veniēbat — ita amābat rem pūblicam. 2.Studium legendī librōs numquam eum dēseruit, nē in exsiliō quidem. 3.Ad discendum in Academiā philosophī ōlim conveniēbant — et nunc legimus quae dīxērunt. 4.Philosophia aptissima est ad animum ōrnandum atque ad vītam bene agendum. 5.Multī ōtiō dant operam; at Cicerō in scrībendō fructum ōtiī inveniēbat. 6.Cupīdō sciendī, ut ait Cicerō, hominem ā bēstiīs dīvidit. 7.Dē philosophiā scrībendō, ōtiō frūctus est et tamen semper negōtium gessit. 8.Ars bene dīcendī et ars bene vīvendī separārī nōn possunt.
Gloss: cotīdiē — daily · dēseruit — abandoned (perf. of dēserō) · nē…quidem — not even · ad discendum — for the purpose of learning (acc. gerund after ad) · ōrnandum / agendum — acc. gerunds after ad · dant operam (+ dat.) — apply themselves to · fructum / frūctus — fruit, reward · sciendī — gen. gerund of sciō · bēstiīs — animals · frūctus est — he enjoyed (deponent perf. of fruor + abl.) · gessit — conducted (perf. of gerō)
📚 PART 2 · LITERARY NOTE 6 min
📜 THE GERUND AND CICERONIAN RHYTHM

Cicero favors ad + gerund and causā/grātiā + gerund because they are concise and rhythmically satisfying. Notice how lines 4 and 8 use parallel gerund phrases for balance:

…ad animum ōrnandum … ad vītam bene agendum (line 4)

ars bene dīcendī et ars bene vīvendī (line 8)

This matched, weighted parallelism is characteristic of Ciceronian periodic style — the gerund is one of his favorite tools for building it.

✎ PART 4 · COMMIT, THEN REVEAL 8 min

Translate three of Cicero's sentences

Write your own translation in the box before you reveal — committing first is what makes the check stick.

Multī ōtiō dant operam; at Cicerō in scrībendō fructum ōtiī inveniēbat.
Many apply themselves to leisure; but Cicero used to find the reward of leisure in writing. — scrībendō = ablative gerund of scrībō after in; dant operam + dative is a fixed idiom, “to devote oneself to.”
Cupīdō sciendī, ut ait Cicerō, hominem ā bēstiīs dīvidit.
The desire of knowing, as Cicero says, separates a human being from the beasts. — sciendī = genitive gerund of sciō, depending on cupīdō, a noun that governs the genitive.
Ars bene dīcendī et ars bene vīvendī separārī nōn possunt.
The art of speaking well and the art of living well cannot be separated. — dīcendī / vīvendī = genitive gerunds depending on ars; bene is an adverb modifying each gerund (verbal force at work).
