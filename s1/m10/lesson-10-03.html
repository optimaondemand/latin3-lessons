★ Causā, Grātiā, and the Gerund at Work
Lesson · about 40 minutes
📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
The forms are yours. Today they turn into the two constructions you will meet on nearly every page of Cicero.
The genitive gerund with causā or grātiā — “for the sake of …-ing” — is one of the most productive patterns in Ciceronian prose. You will also see that the gerund, though a noun, can still take a direct object, keeping its verbal force. Alongside the syntax you learn the module's vocabulary of study, leisure, business, and desire — the very words Cicero uses to weigh the active life against the contemplative one.

📚 PART 1 · VOCABULARY 8 min

Module 10 words — action, study, and leisure

These eight words carry Cicero's argument about how a life should be spent. Note especially the two “for the sake of” words, causā and grātiā, which govern the genitive gerund.

studium, -ī (n.)
eagerness, enthusiasm, pursuit
cupīdō, -inis (f.)
desire, longing (+ genitive)
ōtium, -ī (n.)
leisure, rest, retirement from public life
negōtium, -ī (n.)
business, occupation (nec + ōtium)
causa, -ae (f.) [+ gen.]
reason, cause; causā (+gen.) = for the sake of
grātiā (+gen.)
for the sake of, out of regard for
aptus, -a, -um (+ad/dat.)
suited to, fit for
ratiō, -ōnis (f.)
reason, method, reasoning
🌱 WORD BUILDING · NEC + ŌTIUM

Negōtium is literally “not-leisure” (nec + ōtium) — business is defined by the Romans as the absence of rest. English negotiate and negotiation descend from it. Ratiō, meanwhile, gives us ratio, rational, and reason itself.

📌 PART 2 · THE KEY CONSTRUCTIONS 14 min

Two patterns to know cold

📜 CONSTRUCTION 1 · CAUSĀ / GRĀTIĀ + GENITIVE GERUND

Causā and grātiā take the genitive and follow it: a genitive gerund + causā/grātiā means “for the sake of …-ing.”

Dīcendī causā vēnit.

He came for the sake of speaking. — dīcendī gen. gerund + causā (postposed, governing the genitive).

This is among the most common gerund patterns in Cicero — a concise, rhythmically satisfying way to express purpose.

📜 CONSTRUCTION 2 · THE GERUND WITH AN OBJECT

Because the gerund keeps its verbal force, it can take an accusative object:

cupīdō dīcendī vērum

a desire of speaking the truth — dīcendī gen. gerund; vērum its accusative object.

When the gerund takes an object, Latin frequently prefers the gerundive construction instead (you meet it in Module 11). But the gerund-with-object does occur, especially in the genitive and ablative — so you must be able to read it.

⚖️ DON'T CONFUSE THE PURPOSE MARKERS

Latin expresses purpose with the gerund in two rival ways: ad + accusative gerund (ad dīcendum) and genitive gerund + causā/grātiā (dīcendī causā). Both mean “in order to speak.” The case is your signal for which one you are reading.
