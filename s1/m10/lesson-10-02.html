📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
Yesterday you grasped the idea. Today you build the forms — and they are gratifyingly regular.
Every gerund in Latin is made the same way: take the present stem, insert -nd-, and add second-declension neuter endings. One rule covers all four conjugations and even deponents. Once the forms are automatic, the real work of the module — reading Cicero — is a matter of recognizing which case is doing which job. That case chart is the heart of today.

📚 PART 2 · ONE RULE, EVERY CONJUGATION 12 min

Present stem + -nd- + neuter endings

Drop -re from the present infinitive to reach the present stem, then add -ndī / -ndō / -ndum / -ndō. The same rule runs through all four conjugations — and deponents follow it too.

ConjugationVerbGerund stemGenitive
1stamō, amāreamānd-amandī
2ndmoneō, monēremonend-monendī

💡 THE 3RD/4TH CONNECTING VOWEL

In 3rd i-stem and 4th conjugation, an -ie- appears before -nd- (capiendī, audiendī). Third-conjugation consonant stems show a short -e- (regendī). Note also that a deponent verb, though passive in form, produces an ordinary active-meaning gerund: loquendī = “of speaking.”

📌 PART 3 · THE CASE CHART 8 min

Four forms, four jobs

Here is the model gerund of amō, with the primary use of each case. Memorize the job attached to each form — that is what lets you read Cicero.

CaseFormPrimary useExample
Nominative—use the infinitive (amāre bonum est)—
Genitiveamandīdepends on a noun; after causā / grātiācupīdō amandī
Dativeamandōpurpose; after adjectives of fitnessaptus amandō

⚖️ WATCH THE TWIN FORMS

Dative and ablative are identical in form: both amandō. You tell them apart only by context — a preposition (in, dē) or a sense of means/manner marks the ablative; an adjective of fitness (aptus) or a bare purpose marks the dative. This ambiguity is normal for second-declension neuters and rewards careful reading.
