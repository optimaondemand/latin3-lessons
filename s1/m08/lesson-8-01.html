📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
A single Latin conjunction that can mean when, since, or although — and only context and mood tell you which.
You have spent five modules building the subjunctive. This week that machinery pays off: the conjunction cum introduces four different clause types, and distinguishing them is one of the classic tests of a Latin reader. Today there is no paradigm to memorize — just one governing idea. Cum + indicative is simple time (“when…”). Cum + subjunctive is one of three richer relationships — historical, causal, or concessive — and by Friday you will read all four alive in Petronius's werewolf tale.

Cum clauses lean most heavily on the two past subjunctives — imperfect and pluperfect — because so much of the interesting cum usage lives in narrative.

📚 PART 2 · THE BIG IDEA 12 min

Mood is the first fork in the road

The word cum looks harmless — three letters — but it does the work of three different English conjunctions. The key is never the word itself; it is the combination of verb mood plus context. Start every cum clause by asking one question: indicative or subjunctive?

Cum + indicative

Cum Rōmae erat, multa vīdit.
When he was in Rome, he saw many things.

Simple, neutral time. One event locates another. Translate cum as “when.”

Cum + subjunctive

Cum haec dīxisset, abiit.
When he had said these things, he left.

A richer relationship — circumstance, cause, or concession. Context tells you which.

So the subjunctive is a signpost: it warns you that cum is doing more than marking time. Tomorrow you learn the three subjunctive types by name — historical, causal, and concessive. Today, simply train your eye to make the first cut: is the verb in the cum clause indicative or subjunctive?

💡 WHY LATIN DOES THIS

English uses different words — when, since, although — to signal these relationships. Latin economizes: it keeps one conjunction and lets mood carry the load the extra words would carry in English. That is why a Latin reader must think about mood before meaning.

Commit first, then check: a friend says “cum always just means when.” In one sentence, why is that wrong? Say your answer aloud, then reveal.

Cum means “when” only when it takes the indicative (or sets a plain scene). With the subjunctive it can also mean “since” (causal) or “although” (concessive) — so the mood, not the word, decides the meaning.
