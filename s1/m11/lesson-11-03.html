Lesson 11.03
★ By Whom? — The Dative of Agent
Lesson · about 40 minutes
📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
If something must be done, someone must do it. Today you learn how Latin names that someone.
Two constructions separate honors readers from the rest. First, the dative of agent: in the passive periphrastic, the person obligated goes in the dative, not the usual ā/ab + ablative. Second, the elegant Latin habit of letting a gerundive replace a gerund + object. Master both today and you will read Cicero's clauses of duty exactly as a Roman would.

📚 PART 2 · THE CRITICAL RULE 11 min

Dative of agent

⚠️ THE RULE HONORS READERS MUST OWN

In the passive periphrastic, the person who bears the obligation stands in the dative — the dative of agent — with no preposition. Never use ā/ab + ablative here, even though that is how ordinary passives mark their agent.

Mihi hoc faciendum est. — literally “This is to-be-done for/by me” = “I must do this.”

LatinLiteralIdiomatic English
Mihi labor faciendus est.the work is to-be-done for meI must do the work
Cicerōnī rēs pūblica dēfendenda erat.the republic was to-be-defended for CiceroCicero had to defend the republic
Nōbīs pāx quaerenda est.peace is to-be-sought for uswe must seek peace
💡 A TRANSLATOR'S MOVE

The cleanest English almost always flips the passive periphrastic into the active: make the dative-of-agent person the subject and use “must.” Nōbīs pāx quaerenda est → not the wooden “peace is to be sought by us” but “we must seek peace.”

📌 PART 3 · GERUNDIVE FOR GERUND 9 min

The elegant substitution

When a gerund would take an accusative object, Latin usually prefers a different construction: it drops the gerund and puts a gerundive that agrees with that object. Both say the same thing; the gerundive version is far more common, especially in the accusative and ablative.

Gerund + object (rarer)

ad regendum exercitum
for commanding the army

Gerund regendum + accusative object exercitum.

Gerundive agreeing (preferred)

ad exercitum regendum
for commanding the army

Gerundive regendum now agrees with exercitum (acc. sg. m.).

Note the giveaway: in ad exercitum regendum the -nd- word matches its noun in gender, number, and case. Translate it as if it were still a gerund + object — “for commanding the army,” not the literal “for the army to be commanded.”
