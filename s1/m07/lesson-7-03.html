You now own all three great embedded constructions. The skill that separates a strong reader from a struggling one is telling them apart on sight.

This is applied grammar: no new forms, just sharper eyes and the vocabulary put to work.

⏰ PART 1 · THE DIAGNOSTIC GRID 10 min

All three, side by side

Latin has three major constructions for a clause that serves as the object or complement of a verb. By now you have all three. Each has a single unmistakable signal:

ConstructionKey signalVerb typeExample
Indirect Statementacc. + infinitive (no ut, no interrogative)saying, thinking, knowing, perceivingDīxit eōs vēnisse.
Indirect Questioninterrogative word + subjunctive (no ut)asking, knowing, perceiving, doubtingRogāvit cūr vēnissent.
Indirect Commandut / nē + subjunctive (no interrogative)ordering, urging, asking, persuadingImperāvit ut venīrent.
💡 A THREE-QUESTION TRIAGE

Ask in order: (1) Is there an accusative + infinitive with no ut? → indirect statement. (2) Is there an interrogative word (quid, cūr, num, quandō…) + subjunctive? → indirect question. (3) Is there a plain ut or nē + subjunctive after a verb of ordering? → indirect command.

📌 PART 2 · THE IMPOSTOR: PURPOSE vs. COMMAND 10 min

Same ut, different job

Here is where careful readers earn their keep. An indirect command and a purpose clause both use ut + subjunctive and look identical. The difference lies entirely in the main verb.

Indirect command

Imperāvit ut venīrent.
“He ordered that they come.”

Main verb orders / urges → the ut clause reports what was commanded.

Purpose clause

Cucurrit ut vidēret.
“He ran in order to see.”

Main verb is an action / motion → the ut clause states why he acted.

The test is one question: Is the ut clause reporting a command, or explaining the purpose of an action? A verb of ordering (imperō, rogō, persuādeō, moneō, ōrō…) points to indirect command; a verb of doing or going (vēnit, fēcit, cucurrit…) points to purpose.

⚖️ ONE SENTENCE, BOTH AT ONCE

Advanced Latin cheerfully stacks them: Imperāvit servīs ut cantārent ut convīvae oblectārentur — “He ordered the slaves to sing (indirect command) so that the guests might be entertained (purpose).” Two ut clauses, two different jobs, sorted only by what each attaches to.

💭 QUICK CHECK-IN · no wrong answer
Which diagnostic feels most reliable to you right now?

Reason it out: Ōrāvit eōs ut manērent. Name the construction, and say which word forced your decision. Then reveal.

Indirect command. The main verb ōrāvit (“he begged”) is a verb of requesting, so the ut manērent clause reports what he begged them to do — not a purpose. Note eōs is accusative, because ōrō takes the accusative of the person.
