★ The Formula — and the One Verb That Breaks It
Lesson · about 40 minutes
📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
Yesterday you learned the silhouette. Today you learn every rule that fills it in.
Three things make an indirect command precise: the sequence of tenses (which subjunctive tense to use), the case the person commanded takes (dative or accusative, depending on the verb), and the great exception — iubeō, which builds a command with accusative + infinitive instead of ut. Master these and you can both parse and produce the construction.

2

3
I will be able to recognize that iubeō takes accusative + infinitive, not ut + subjunctive.

📚 PART 2 · RULE 1: THE FORMULA 14 min

Verb of commanding + ut / nē + subjunctive

A verb of commanding, urging, or requesting is followed by ut (positive) or nē (negative) and the subjunctive. The sequence of tenses governs which subjunctive: after a primary main verb (present, future, future perfect) use the present subjunctive; after a secondary main verb (imperfect, perfect, pluperfect) use the imperfect subjunctive.

Primary sequence

Imperat ut veniant.
“He orders that they come.”

Present main verb → present subjunctive (veniant).

Secondary sequence

Imperāvit ut venīrent.
“He ordered that they come.”

Perfect main verb → imperfect subjunctive (venīrent).

The person who is commanded takes a case that depends on the governing verb. Learn this table — it is the most common source of error in the construction:

Introducing verbPerson commandedConnector + subjunctive
imperōdativeut / nē + subj.
persuādeōdativeut / nē + subj.
rogō, ōrōaccusativeut / nē + subj.
moneō, hortoraccusativeut / nē + subj.
prohibeōaccusativenē / quōminus + subj.
💡 DATIVE OR ACCUSATIVE?

A useful memory hook: imperō and persuādeō already govern the dative in their basic meanings (“give an order to someone,” “make something agreeable to someone”), so the person stays dative here too. The verbs of asking and warning (rogō, ōrō, moneō, hortor) take a plain accusative object.

📌 PART 3 · RULE 2: THE iubeō EXCEPTION 8 min

iubeō refuses ut

Iubeō (“to order”) is a verb of commanding — but it does not take ut + subjunctive. Instead it takes the accusative + infinitive, the indirect-statement construction:

Iussit eōs venīre.

“He ordered them to come.” — eōs accusative subject of the infinitive · venīre present infinitive. No ut, no subjunctive.

Both imperāvit eīs ut venīrent and iussit eōs venīre mean “he ordered them to come.” Same idea, two different constructions. Do not blend them: never write iussit ut… or imperāvit eōs venīre.

⚖️ WHY TWO PATTERNS?

Latin treats iubeō and vetō (“to forbid”) as verbs whose object is the whole action — hence accusative + infinitive, as if reporting a fact one wants realized. The ut-verbs, by contrast, frame a command as a wish directed at a person, which the subjunctive of will naturally expresses. Recognizing that difference is the mark of an advanced reader.
