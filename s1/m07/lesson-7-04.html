⏰ PART 1 · MEET THE AUTHOR 6 min
🏛️ PETRONIUS & THE CENA TRIMALCHIONIS

Petronius (d. 66 CE), a courtier of Nero known as the arbiter elegantiae (“judge of taste”), wrote the Satyrica, a sprawling comic novel. Its most famous episode is the Cena Trimalchiōnis — the “Dinner of Trimalchio” — a lavish banquet thrown by a fabulously wealthy former slave.

Trimalchio is nouveau riche in the extreme: vulgar, boastful, and desperate to display his fortune. Petronius lets us laugh at him — and then, quietly, uncomfortably, lets us see what absolute wealth and power look like at the dinner table.

📚 PART 2 · THE PASSAGE 16 min

Acroāma Trīmalchiōnis — adapted from Petronius, Satyrica 52–55

Read each line aloud. For every sentence, find the governing verb of commanding, the connector (ut or nē), and the subjunctive verb. Watch line 5 — it is the iubeō exception.

1.Trīmalchiō servīs imperāvit ut saltārent et cantārent ut convīvae oblectārentur.
2.Monuit eōs nē quid frangerent, nē damnum paterētur.
3.Ōrāvit hospitēs ut manērent et nē tam citō discēdere cōnārentur.
4.Interim acroāma intrāvit; Trīmalchiō persuāsit convīvīs ut audīrent.
5.Iussit puerōs splendōrem argentī ostendere ut omnēs mīrārentur.
6.Nē quis abīret, imperāvit iānitōrī ut ōstium clauderet.
7.Monuit quoque convīvās ut modo biberent, nē nimium vīnī caperent.
8.Sed nēmō eī persuādēre poterat ut ipse quiēsceret — plūs omnibus bibit.
Gloss: saltārent — they might dance (impf. subj. of saltō) · oblectārentur — might be entertained (passive impf. subj. of oblectō) · frangerent — they might break (impf. subj.) · damnum paterētur — he might suffer loss (deponent patior) · acroāma — entertainment act (Greek loan-word, acc.) · iussit puerōs…ostendere — ordered the boys to show (iubeō + acc. + inf., Rule 2) · iānitōrī — to the doorman (dative) · ōstium clauderet — might close the door · modo — only, merely (here “drink only a little”) · quiēsceret — might rest
⚖️ LITERARY NOTE · THE CONTROLLED FEAST

One of Petronius's darkest ironies in chs. 52–60 is that Trimalchio, who hosts a feast supposedly in his guests' honor, controls every moment of it through commands. The passage is dominated by verbs of ordering (imperāvit, monuit, ōrāvit, persuāsit, iussit) — all followed by indirect commands. The guests eat, drink, and stay or go entirely at Trimalchio's directive. The feast is generous only on the surface; underneath, it is an exercise in absolute household power. And the final line delivers the punchline: the man who commands everyone can command no one to make himself stop.

📝 PART 4 · COMMIT, THEN REVEAL 6 min

Translate three, then check yourself

Write out a full translation of each line before you reveal — the honest attempt is where the learning happens.

1. Trīmalchiō servīs imperāvit ut saltārent et cantārent ut convīvae oblectārentur.
“Trimalchio ordered his slaves to dance and sing, so that the guests might be entertained.” — The first ut is an indirect command after imperāvit (with servīs dative); the second ut is a purpose clause explaining why they should perform. Two different ut-constructions in one sentence.
5. Iussit puerōs splendōrem argentī ostendere ut omnēs mīrārentur.
“He ordered the boys to display the splendor of the silver, so that everyone might marvel.” — iussit + acc. + infinitive (Rule 2: iubeō takes indirect statement, not an ut-clause). The ut omnēs mīrārentur clause here is purpose, not indirect command.
8. Sed nēmō eī persuādēre poterat ut ipse quiēsceret — plūs omnibus bibit.
“But no one was able to persuade him that he himself should rest — he drank more than everyone.” — persuādēre + dative (eī) + ut + subjunctive = indirect command. The punchline: Trimalchio controls everyone else, but has no self-control.
