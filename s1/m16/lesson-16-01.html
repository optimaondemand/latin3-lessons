What the S1 final actually asks

📜 SEMESTER 1 FINAL · TWO PARTS

Part 1 — Unseen translation. An 8–10 line passage from Cicero or Petronius, with only minimal glosses. You will not have seen it before. Success depends on mapping every subordinate clause before you commit to a translation.

Part 2 — Literary / cultural analysis essay. A discursive argument about the authors and their world — strongest when it quotes the Latin and shows how a specific construction produces a specific effect.

💡 HOW M16 MAPS ONTO THE EXAM

The review guide in the Finals folder carries the full construction list. This week's Translation Assignment is your closest pre-finals rehearsal for Part 1; the literary work across Days 2–4 rehearses Part 2. Treat the Translation Assignment as a mock exam, not homework.

✅ PART 3 · THE SEMESTER ARC · ORIENT YOURSELF 15 min

Three authors, one conversation

📚 PLAUTUS → PETRONIUS → CICERO

Plautus (c. 254–184 BC): Comedy; colloquial, punning, energetic. Subjunctives are natural — commands, wishes, purpose — unforced. Characters speak as Roman crowds spoke.

Petronius (died AD 66): Satire; the Satyricon parodies elevated Latin while writing in it. Trimalchio's party lampoons the language of the novī hominēs. Grammar is sometimes deliberately wrong.

Cicero (106–43 BC): The canonical standard — periodic, hierarchically organized, every subjunctive in its place. Reading Cicero after Plautus and Petronius reveals how deliberately each author deployed the same grammatical system for completely different rhetorical ends.

Commit first, then reveal: why read Cicero last, after Plautus and Petronius, rather than first? Frame an answer in a sentence or two, then check one way to say it.

Because Cicero is the standard, and a standard means most when you have already heard the alternatives. Having read Plautus bend the subjunctive to the rhythms of the street and Petronius break grammar on purpose, you can see that Cicero's periodic control is a choice — one deployment of a shared system, not the only possible Latin. Reading him last turns "correct Latin" from a rule into a rhetorical decision.
