📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
Yesterday you placed the three authors. Today you hear the difference between them — in the grammar itself.
Plautus, Petronius, and Cicero all inherited the same set of constructions: subjunctives of purpose, result, and command; conditions; relative clauses of characteristic; indirect statement. What separates them is not the toolkit but the use. Today you connect each author's temperament to the way he wields that shared system — the exact skill Part 2 of the final rewards.

📚 PART 2 · THREE TEMPERAMENTS, ONE TOOLKIT 14 min

How each author bends the shared grammar

You met all of these constructions across M01–M15. What changes from author to author is tone and density — how thickly the subjunctives are packed and to what end.

AuthorRegisterHow the subjunctive lands
PlautusColloquial comedyHortatory and jussive subjunctives, wishes, purpose — unforced, as spoken. Grammar serves the joke.
PetroniusSatirical proseElevated constructions mixed with deliberate error in freedmen's mouths — grammar is the satire.
CiceroPeriodic oratoryEvery subjunctive subordinated and placed — result, characteristic, condition — the sentence architected top to bottom.
⚖️ THE POINT OF THE ARC

Reading Cicero after Plautus and Petronius reveals how deliberately each author deployed the same grammatical system for completely different rhetorical ends. Correct Latin is not a ceiling everyone strains toward; it is one register among several, chosen or refused for effect.

📌 PART 3 · THE PERIODIC SENTENCE 8 min

Cicero's signature

A period is a long sentence whose sense is suspended until the end: subordinate clauses stack and interlock, the main verb held back so that meaning arrives all at once with the close. It demands that a reader hold every open clause in mind — which is exactly why mapping subordination before translating is the whole game on the exam.

Loose (paratactic)

Clauses laid side by side, each complete: and… and… then… Sense arrives piece by piece. Closer to Plautus's stage rhythm.

Periodic (hypotactic)

Clauses nested and subordinated, main verb withheld to the end. Sense arrives whole, on the last word. The Ciceronian standard.
💡 READING STRATEGY

In a period, find the main verb first — it is often near the end. Then work outward from it, slotting each subordinate clause into its role. Never translate left-to-right through a Ciceronian period; map it, then render it.
