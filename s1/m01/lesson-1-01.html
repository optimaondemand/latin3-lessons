★ Verba Dēpōnentia — Verbs That Wear a Disguise
Lesson · about 35 minutes
📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
Some Latin verbs look passive their whole lives — and never once mean it. This week you learn to see through the disguise.
You already know the passive voice: passive endings, and the subject has something done to it. But Latin has a whole class of verbs that take passive forms in every tense and person and yet mean something active. They are deponent verbs — from dēpōnere, “to put aside.” They have put their active forms aside entirely; the passive forms are all they have. Today is orientation: no drilling, just one large idea and why it matters for every author you will read this semester.
🎯 BY THE END OF THIS LESSON YOU WILL BE ABLE TO…
1
I will be able to define a deponent verb: passive in form, active in meaning, with no active forms at all.
2
I will be able to explain why misreading a deponent as a true passive derails an entire sentence.
⏰ PART 1 · WARM-UP 6 min

What you already know about the passive

In Latin 2 you learned the passive personal endings that attach to the present system: -r, -ris, -tur, -mur, -minī, -ntur. A form like amātur means “he/she is loved.” Hold that in mind — deponents borrow exactly these endings but refuse the passive meaning.

Puer laudātur.

The boy is praised. — a genuine passive: the subject receives the action.

Mīles mīrātur.

The soldier marvels. — identical ending, but this verb (mīror) is deponent, so the meaning is active.

💭 QUICK CHECK-IN · no wrong answer
Looking at those two sentences, what makes deponents tricky at first?
📚 PART 2 · THE BIG IDEA 12 min

The deponent principle

A deponent verb has passive forms but active meaning. There is no active form to fall back on — the verb simply does not have one. When a deponent appears in a Latin sentence, you translate it as active, always.

True passive

Hostēs verentur.
If this were passive: “The enemies are feared.”

But vereor is deponent — so this is wrong.

Deponent — the real reading

Hostēs verentur.
“The enemies fear / are in awe.”

Passive form, active meaning. The subject does the fearing.

Deponents are not a rare curiosity. They appear constantly in the authors you meet this semester — Plautus, Petronius, Cicero. A reader who mistakes mīrantur for a passive (“they are marveled”) instead of a deponent (“they marvel”) will misread every sentence that contains one. Fluent reading depends on recognizing them on sight.

💡 WHY “PUT ASIDE”?

The name comes from dēpōnere, “to lay down, put aside.” Grammarians imagined these verbs as having set aside their active endings and kept only the passive ones — while holding on to active meaning. The disguise is total: form says passive, sense says active.
