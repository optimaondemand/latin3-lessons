★ The Present System — Same Endings, Active Sense
Lesson · about 40 minutes
📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
Yesterday you learned the disguise. Today you learn to read every fold of it — present, imperfect, and future.
Deponents follow the passive conjugation patterns you already know. The endings are identical to the Latin 2 passive; only the meaning changes. Today you work through the present-system paradigms for 1st and 2nd conjugation deponents, then lock in the three rules that let you identify a deponent from its dictionary entry and read it without hesitation.

📚 PART 2 · THE PARADIGMS 14 min

Present system, two conjugations

Compare these to your Latin 2 passive forms — the endings are the same (-or, -ris, -tur, -mur, -minī, -ntur and their imperfect and future counterparts). Only the translation is different: it is always active.

1st conj. — mīror (to marvel at)

SingularPlural
1stmīror — I marvelmīrāmur — we marvel
2ndmīrāris — you marvelmīrāminī — you (pl.) marvel
3rdmīrātur — he/she marvelsmīrantur — they marvel
impf.mīrābar — I was marvelingmīrābāmur — we were marveling
fut.mīrābor — I will marvelmīrābimur — we will marvel

2nd conj. — vereor (to fear)

SingularPlural
1stvereor — I fearverēmur — we fear
2ndverēris — you fearverēminī — you (pl.) fear
3rdverētur — he/she fearsverentur — they fear
impf.verēbar — I was fearingverēbāmur — we were fearing
fut.verēbor — I will fearverēbimur — we will fear
💡 NOTICE

Every single form ends in a passive marker — and every single English gloss is active. That mismatch is the deponent. Train your eye to expect it, not to be surprised by it.

📌 PART 3 · THE THREE RULES 9 min

How to know a deponent when you meet one

RULE 1 · THE DEPONENT PRINCIPLE

A deponent verb has passive forms but active meaning. There is no active form to fall back on. If a deponent appears in a Latin sentence, translate it as active. Mīrantur means “they marvel” — never “they are marveled.”

RULE 2 · HOW TO IDENTIFY IN A TEXT

Look up the verb's dictionary entry. If the 1st principal part ends in -or and the infinitive ends in -ārī (1st), -ērī (2nd), -ī (3rd), -īrī (4th), it is a deponent. The perfect principal part ends in -us/a/um sum (active participle + sum), not -us/a/um est in a fixed 3rd-person way.

RULE 3 · SEMI-DEPONENTS (PREVIEW)

A few verbs are normal in the present system but deponent in the perfect system. Audeō, -ēre, ausus sum (to dare) is the most common. In the perfect, ausus sum means “I dared” (not “I was dared”). Semi-deponents are introduced fully in M02.

✅ PART 4 · WORK THE FORMS 13 min

Five reps — building forms, reading endings, and applying the rules. Keep the paradigms above in view if you need them; wrong answers give a hint.

✅ TRY IT · read the ending

✅ TRY IT · the future

✅ TRY IT · is it a deponent?
A dictionary entry reads hortor, hortārī, hortātus sum. What does this tell you?
✅ TRY IT · semi-deponent
Audeō, audēre, ausus sum is a semi-deponent. Where does it behave passively in form?

Commit, then reveal: conjugate the imperfect 3rd singular of vereor and translate it. Say the form and its meaning out loud before checking.
