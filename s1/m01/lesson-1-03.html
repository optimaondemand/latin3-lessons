📚 PART 1 · VOCABULĀRIUM 10 min

Eight words for Module 01

All seven verbs are deponents. The principal parts show 1st singular present · infinitive · 1st singular perfect. The perfect is formed with a perfect active participle + sum, even though the verb is “passive” in form.

1st conj. deponent · + inf.
hortor, hortārī, hortātus sum
to urge, encourage
1st conj. deponent · + ut/inf.
moror, morārī, morātus sum
to delay, linger
1st conj. deponent
polliceor, pollicērī, pollicitus sum
to promise
2nd conj. deponent · + acc. or inf.

2nd conj. deponent · + acc. or nē

💡 READ THE ENTRY LIKE A DIAGNOSTIC

Every verb here shows the deponent signature: 1st part in -or, infinitive in -ārī or -ērī, perfect in -us sum. Notice the infinitive is what separates 1st (-ārī) from 2nd (-ērī) conjugation.

📌 PART 2 · THE DEPONENT PERFECT 12 min

Looks passive, means active

The perfect tense of a deponent uses a perfect active participle (PAP) + a form of esse. This looks identical to a passive perfect. But remember: the meaning is always active.

FormLooks likeMeans
mīrātus sumPassive perfect: “I have been marveled”I have marveled / I marveled
hortātus esPassive perfect: “you have been urged”You have urged / you urged
pollicitus estPassive perfect: “he has been promised”He has promised / he promised
⚠️ COMMON MISTAKE

Students often translate a deponent perfect as passive because of the -tus/a/um sum pattern. The test: check the dictionary entry. If the verb's principal parts show -or, -ārī/-ērī/-ī, -ātus/-ītus/-tus sum, it is a deponent and the meaning is always active regardless of form.

One more subtlety worth noting as an honors reader: because the participle agrees with the subject in gender and number, mīrāta est tells you the marveler is feminine singular, and hortātī sunt tells you the urgers are masculine plural. The “passive” participle carries real information — just not passive meaning.
