⏰ PART 1 · LECTIO 14 min

Mīles Glōriōsus, Act I (adapted)

Plautus's play opens with the braggart soldier Pyrgopolȳnicēs (whose name means something like “Destroyer of towers”) boasting in the forum while his parasite Artōtrōgus (literally “Bread-chewer”) flatters him. His clever slave Palaestrio watches from a distance. Read the passage carefully, and identify every deponent verb before reading the gloss.

1. Pyrgopolȳnicēs, mīles glōriōsus, per forum ambulābat et magnās rēs pollicēbātur. 2. “Omnēs mē mīrantur,” inquit, “et rēgēs ipsī mē verentur.” 3. Artōtrōgus, parāsītus, mīlitem hortābātur: “Ita vērō, domine — glōria tua caelum tangit!” 4. Sed Palaestrio, servus callidus, post columnam morābātur et haec audiēbat. 5. Sēcum cōgitābat: “Hic stultus nimis sibi cōnfīdit. Ego tamen cōnābor eum fallere.”
Gloss: ambulābat — was walking · rēs (acc. pl.) — things, deeds · inquit — says, said (defective verb) · ipsī — themselves, even (intensive adj.) · parāsītus — parasite, hanger-on · ita vērō — yes indeed, exactly so · domine — master, sir (voc.) · caelum — sky, heaven · tangit — touches · post + acc. — behind · columnam — column, pillar · callidus — clever, shrewd · sēcum — with himself (= sē + cum) · stultus — fool, foolish · nimis — too much, excessively · cōnfīdit — trusts (semi-deponent in perfect only; present is active here) · fallere — to deceive (inf.)

The deponents to find: pollicēbātur (was promising), mīrantur (marvel), verentur (fear/respect), hortābātur (was urging), morābātur (was lingering), cōnābor (I will try). Six deponents in five sentences — this is why they must be automatic.

📚 PART 2 · LITERARY & CULTURAL NOTE 11 min
🎭 PLAUTUS AND SOCIAL COMEDY

Plautus wrote for a popular Roman audience in the 200s BCE. His plots are almost always the same: a clever slave outwits a pompous or stupid authority figure. In Mīles Glōriōsus, it is the slave Palaestrio who controls the action while the braggart soldier believes himself to be in charge. This inversion of social order is the engine of Roman comedy — and it explains why deponents like hortor, cōnor, and moror appear so often: characters in Plautus are constantly urging, scheming, and delaying.

⚖️ THE THEME-WORD: GLŌRIA

The play's title, Mīles Glōriōsus, means “The Boastful Soldier” — and glōria is the hinge. In its noble sense it is glory earned; in Pyrgopolȳnicēs's mouth it is hollow self-praise. Watch how Plautus lets the soldier claim that even kings verentur (fear/respect) him, while the audience knows a single slave will unmake him. The gap between claimed glōria and real worth is the whole joke.
