📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
Yesterday you learned to take a condition apart. Today you learn to name what you're holding.
Latin sorts conditions into three families — and one of them (contrary-to-fact) has a present and a past form, so really you're learning four signatures. Each is a distinct pairing of mood and tense across the two clauses. Learn the four signatures today and you can classify almost any condition Cicero or Petronius throws at you.

📚 PART 2 · THE THREE RULES 18 min

Three families, four signatures

RULE 1 — SIMPLE / OPEN CONDITION (INDICATIVE)

Protasis: sī + indicative. Apodosis: indicative. Treats the condition as factually open — the speaker does not commit to whether it is true. Translation: “If you do X, Y will happen” or “If you did X, Y happened.” Sī vēnit, laetus sum — “If he is coming, I am glad.”

RULE 2 — FUTURE LESS VIVID (SHOULD / WOULD)

Protasis: sī + present subjunctive. Apodosis: present subjunctive. Treats the condition as possible but uncertain — a polite or hypothetical “should / would” register. Translation: “If you should do X, Y would happen.” Sī veniat, laetus sim — “If he should come, I would be glad.”

RULE 3 — CONTRARY-TO-FACT

Present contrary-to-fact: sī + imperfect subjunctive, apodosis imperfect subjunctive: “If X were (but it isn't), Y would be.” Past contrary-to-fact: sī + pluperfect subjunctive, apodosis pluperfect subjunctive: “If X had been (but it wasn't), Y would have been.” Look for the -issem or -rem endings in both clauses together.

💡 THE KEY DIAGNOSTIC

The suffix pattern in both clauses identifies the type. Both indicative → simple. Both present subj. → future less vivid. Both impf. subj. → present contrary-to-fact. Both plupf. subj. → past contrary-to-fact. When you see a sī / nisi + verb, check: what is the mood and tense in the protasis? Then confirm the apodosis matches.

✅ TRY IT · hear the register
An English rendering runs “If you should ask, I would answer.” That “should / would” register signals which Latin type?

Classify and translate, out loud: Sī Cicerōnem audīvissēs, persuāsus essēs. Identify both verbs' mood and tense first, then reveal.

Past contrary-to-fact. Audīvissēs and persuāsus essēs are both pluperfect subjunctive → past contrary-to-fact: “If you had heard Cicero, you would have been convinced.” The implication is that the hearing never happened.
