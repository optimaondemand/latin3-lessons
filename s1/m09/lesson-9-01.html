📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
This week you learn one of the most expressive structures in Latin — the conditional sentence, the grammar of possibility, doubt, and regret.
A condition lets a Latin author say not only what is, but what would be, what might be, and what would have been but never was. Today there is no paradigm to drill — just the anatomy: every condition splits into two clauses, and the mood and tense of the verbs decide what kind of condition it is. Master the anatomy now, and the three types you meet tomorrow will fall into place. This module also marks a turning point in your reading: we close the Petronius block and open Cicero.

⏰ PART 1 · RECALL 6 min

You already own the tools

Conditions do not require anything new — they recombine machinery you have already built. You know the indicative (statements of fact) and, from earlier modules, the subjunctive in its four tenses (present, imperfect, perfect, pluperfect). Conditions simply put those moods to work in a two-clause frame.

📚 PART 2 · THE BIG IDEA 14 min

Two clauses, one sentence

A condition has two parts: the protasis (the “if” clause, introduced by sī or nisi) and the apodosis (the “then” clause, the result). The verb moods in both clauses, taken together, determine the condition type.

Protasis — the “if”

Sī vēnit…
If he is coming…

Introduced by sī (“if”) or nisi (“unless”). Sets up the supposition.

Apodosis — the “then”

…laetus sum.
…I am glad.

The result or conclusion. No conjunction needed — Latin just states it.

Here is the honors-level insight: the type of a condition is not signaled by which words are chosen but by which moods and tenses the two verbs carry. Both indicative? One kind of condition. Both present subjunctive? Another. Both imperfect subjunctive? Yet another. You will learn the full menu tomorrow — today, simply train yourself to read the verbs as a matched pair.

💡 WHY READ BOTH VERBS?

A single verb can be ambiguous out of context. But a condition gives you two verbs that Latin expects to agree in construction. Reading them together — protasis verb and apodosis verb as a pair — is your most reliable diagnostic. When they don't quite match, you've found a deliberate stylistic choice (a “mixed condition”), which is exactly the kind of subtlety Cicero exploits.

✅ TRY IT · what decides the type
What determines a condition's type?

Split this sentence into its two parts, out loud: Nisi amīcōs habērem, rem pūblicam servāre nōn possem. Name the protasis, the apodosis, and the trigger word — then reveal.

Protasis: Nisi amīcōs habērem (“unless I had friends”) — trigger word nisi. Apodosis: rem pūblicam servāre nōn possem (“I could not preserve the republic”). Notice both verbs are imperfect subjunctive (habērem…possem) — a matched pair. You'll learn tomorrow that this pairing marks a present contrary-to-fact condition.
