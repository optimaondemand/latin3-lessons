⏰ PART 1 · PASSAGE ONE 14 min

Cicero defines friendship

In De Amicitiā, the aged Laelius recalls his friendship with Scipio Africanus the Younger, and pauses to define what friendship is:

Est enim amīcitia nihil aliud nisi omnium dīvīnārum hūmānārumque rērum cum benevolentiā et cāritāte cōnsēnsiō.
— Cicero, De Amicitiā 20

Work it in pieces — find the verb, the subject, then the great genitive phrase it governs:

LatinParseSense
Est … cōnsēnsiōverb + predicate nom."[it] is … an agreement/harmony"
enimconjunction"for, indeed" — signals an explanation
amīcitianom. sg. (subject)"friendship"
nihil aliud nisifixed idiom"nothing other than"
omnium … rērumgen. pl."of all things"
dīvīnārum hūmānārumquegen. pl. adj."divine and human"
cum benevolentiā et cāritāteabl. (cum + abl.)"with goodwill and affection"
✅ POLISHED TRANSLATION

"For friendship is nothing other than agreement on all things divine and human, joined with goodwill and affection." Notice how the English rearranges the word order Cicero saved for the end — a faithful translation carries the meaning, not the Latin order.

📚 PART 2 · PASSAGE TWO 12 min

Cicero on the power of study

From Pro Archiā, Cicero's defense of the poet Archias — a speech in the charged politics of Pompey's Rome. Here he praises the lifelong companionship of learning:

Haec studia adulēscentiam alunt, senectūtem oblectant, secundās rēs ōrnant, adversīs perfugium ac sōlācium praebent.
— Cicero, Pro Archiā 16

Four parallel clauses, each subject haec studia (these pursuits) driving a verb + object. This balanced structure is called tricolon/quadricolon — a hallmark of Ciceronian rhythm.

ClauseLiteral
adulēscentiam alunt"they nourish youth"
senectūtem oblectant"they delight old age"
secundās rēs ōrnant"they adorn favorable circumstances (prosperity)"
adversīs perfugium ac sōlācium praebent"they provide a refuge and comfort in adversity"
✅ POLISHED TRANSLATION

"These pursuits nourish our youth and delight our old age; they adorn prosperity and offer a refuge and comfort in adversity." The four crisp clauses become two balanced English pairs — faithful to the parallelism, natural to the ear.

Commit before revealing: what are the three moves of a good translation, in order? Say them, then reveal.

1. Find the backbone — locate the main verb and its subject. 2. Map the structure — attach every phrase (genitives, ablatives, objects) to what it modifies. 3. Polish for English — rearrange word order so the meaning reads naturally, while staying faithful. Use exactly this on your own passage.
