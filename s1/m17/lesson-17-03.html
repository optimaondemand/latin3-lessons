★ Finding the Words — Sources & Etymology
Lesson · about 40 minutes
📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
You have a figure. Today you learn to find their words — and to tell a real source from a fake one.
The 55-point Latin Translation and the 15-point Etymology sections both depend on one thing: an authentic primary-source passage. Today you learn what makes a source authentic, where to look, the Latin vocabulary scholars use to talk about sources, and how the etymology section grows straight out of the words you translate.
🎯 BY THE END OF THIS LESSON YOU WILL BE ABLE TO…
1
I will be able to distinguish an authentic primary source from a later summary or paraphrase.
2
I will be able to use the Latin research terms fons, auctor, testimonium, and exemplar correctly.
3
I will be able to trace English derivatives from key Latin words for the etymology section.
⏰ PART 1 · PRIMARY vs. SECONDARY 8 min

What counts as authentic?

A primary source is Latin written by your figure or by a contemporary who witnessed the events — the real words, not a retelling. A secondary source is a later summary, encyclopedia entry, or translation. Your passage must be primary.

Primary — use this

Cicero's own De Amicitiā; Caesar's De Bello Gallico; an inscription; a contemporary letter. Authentic Latin, from the period.

Secondary — do not translate

A Wikipedia summary; a modern textbook paragraph; an English biography; a paraphrase "in the style of." Useful for background — not for the translation.
💡 WHERE TO LOOK

Reliable open collections of authentic Latin include the Latin Library, Perseus Digital Library, and PHI Latin Texts. Search by author, then locate a passage of about 4–8 lines. Record the exact citation (author, work, book/section) — you'll need it.

📚 PART 2 · THE SCHOLAR'S VOCABULARY 10 min

Latin words for talking about Latin

Since antiquity, these are the terms Latinists use to describe sources. Learn them — they belong in your Process Reflection.

LatinMeaningEnglish descendant
fons, fontis (m.)spring; sourcefont, fountain, fontanelle
auctor, auctoris (m.)author; originator; authorityauthor, authority, authorize
testimonium, -ī (n.)witness statement; evidencetestimony, testimonial
exemplar, -āris (n.)copy; model; specimenexemplar, example, sample
verbum, -ī (n.)wordverb, verbal, verbatim
🌱 SOURCE = "SPRING"

Notice the metaphor buried in fons: a text is a spring from which knowledge flows. When we call something a "primary source," we mean water taken straight from the spring — not carried second-hand in someone else's bucket. This is why an authentic passage matters so much.

📌 PART 3 · THE ETYMOLOGY SECTION 6 min

Fifteen points that grow from your passage

Once you have translated your passage, pick a few key words and trace their English descendants. Show the Latin root, its meaning, and the modern English words that grew from it — explaining any shift in sense. This is the etymology component (15 pts), and it comes directly out of the Latin you already worked with.

virtūs → virtue, virtuoso, virtual

Root virtūs = manliness, courage, excellence. English keeps the moral sense in virtue, the skill sense in virtuoso, and stretched it to "in effect" in virtual.

imperāre → empire, imperative, imperial

Root imperāre = to command. A single verb of command seeds a whole family of English power-words.

💭 QUICK CHECK-IN · no wrong answer
Where will you go first to find your figure's authentic Latin?

Commit before you check: pick one key Latin word you expect in your passage and predict two English derivatives. Say them, then reveal a worked example.

Take imperāre (to command). Predicted derivatives: imperative (a command form) and empire (a commanded realm) — plus imperial, emperor. A single verb of command seeds an entire English vocabulary of power. That is exactly the kind of note the etymology section rewards.
