★ When Quī Stops Stating Facts
Lesson · about 35 minutes
📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
You already read ordinary relative clauses fluently. This week you learn what happens when Cicero puts the verb in the subjunctive instead.
A relative clause with the indicative states a plain fact about a specific antecedent: poēta quem amāmus clārus est — “the poet whom we love is famous.” But when the verb turns subjunctive, the clause is no longer reporting a fact — it is characterizing a type, or expressing a purpose. No new paradigm to memorize today; the subjunctive forms are already yours from Modules 6–11. The task this week is learning to read the mood and know what it signals. By Friday you will handle these clauses in adapted Cicero.
🎯 BY THE END OF THIS LESSON YOU WILL BE ABLE TO…
1
I will be able to distinguish an ordinary (indicative) relative clause from one that carries a subjunctive.
2
I will be able to explain, in general terms, why Latin uses the subjunctive to move from stating a fact to characterizing or expressing purpose.

📚 PART 2 · THE BIG IDEA 12 min

Stating vs. characterizing

An ordinary relative clause uses the indicative and states a fact about a specific antecedent. A relative clause with the subjunctive expresses something other than pure fact — characterization, purpose, or result — and its antecedent is often indefinite or generalizing.

Fact — indicative

Poēta quem amāmus clārus est.
The poet whom we love is famous.

A specific poet; the clause reports what is true of him.

Characterization — subjunctive

Nēmō est quī hunc poētam nōn amet.
There is no one who does not love this poet.

No specific person; the clause describes a general character.

This week you meet two of these subjunctive uses — the relative clause of characteristic (“the sort of person who…”) and the relative clause of purpose (“…who would…/ in order to…”). Tomorrow you learn to tell them apart by their signals; today you only need the core instinct: indicative reports, subjunctive characterizes or aims.

💡 WHY YOU ALREADY HAVE THE FORMS

You spent Modules 6–11 building the subjunctive — present, imperfect, and the sequence of tenses. Nothing about the forms is new here. What is new is a fresh trigger for using them: the relative pronoun quī, quae, quod itself, once its antecedent is a type rather than a fact.

✅ TRY IT · the core instinct
A subjunctive inside a relative clause is a signal that the clause is…

Commit first, then reveal. In is est quī semper vērum dīcat, is dīcat indicative or subjunctive — and how do you know? Say your reasoning aloud, then reveal.

Subjunctive. The form is dīcat, not dīcit — present subjunctive of dīcō. Paired with is est quī (“he is the sort of man who…”), it characterizes a type rather than stating a fact about one named person. You will name this pattern — the relative clause of characteristic — tomorrow.
