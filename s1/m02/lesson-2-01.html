⏰ PART 1 · RECALL 5 min

What a deponent already is

A deponent verb has laid aside (Latin dēpōnere) its active forms. It looks passive in every tense — the endings -or, -ris, -tur, -mur, -minī, -ntur — but it means something active. Loquitur is not “he is spoken”; it is “he speaks.”

📚 PART 2 · THE DICTIONARY SIGNAL 12 min

The principal parts give it away

You never have to guess. The very first thing a dictionary shows you — the principal parts — tells you the verb's type.

🔎 DICTIONARY ENTRY SIGNAL

When a verb's first principal part ends in -or (e.g., loquor, patior, orior), the verb is deponent — its forms look passive throughout, but you translate them actively. When the first principal part is active but the perfect ends in -us sum (e.g., audeō … ausus sum), the verb is semi-deponent.

Deponent — passive all through

loquor, loquī, locūtus sum
to speak

First part -or. Passive forms in every tense; active meaning everywhere.

Semi-deponent — split personality

audeō, audēre, ausus sum
to dare

Active present system; deponent (passive-form) perfect system.

Contrast both with an ordinary verb like portō, portāre, portāvī, portātus: active first part, active perfect. When such a verb does wear a passive ending, it really is passive — portātur = “it is carried.” Only for deponents does a passive ending hide an active meaning.
