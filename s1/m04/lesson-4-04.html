⏰ PART 1 · PLAUTUS FINALE 12 min

Pyrgopol&ymacr;nicēs Dīmittitur

Read each line aloud. Find the main verb, then check for an intensity word before you decide whether an ut clause is purpose or result. The gloss is below — try each line yourself first.

PYRGOPOL&Ymacr;NICĒS DĪMITTITUR — ADAPTED FROM PLAUTUS, MĪLES GLŌRIŌSUS FINALE
1.Palaestrio Pleusiclēn hortātus est ut Philocōmasiam quam celerrimē abdūceret.
2.Ita omnia parāta erant ut mīles nihil suspicārētur.
3.Pyrgopol&ymacr;nicēs, glōriā captus, nē quid malī sibi accideret timēbat.
4.Tandem Palaestrio eum monuit ut Philocōmasiam ā sē dīmitteret.
5.Mīles ita stultus erat ut sine morā cōnsentīret et glōriāns exīret.
Gloss: abdūceret — would lead away (impf. subj., purpose) · parāta erant — had been prepared · suspicārētur — would suspect (impf. subj. of suspicor, a deponent!) · dīmitteret — would release, let go · morā — delay (abl.) · cōnsentīret — would agree · glōriāns — boasting (pres. act. participle)

Note the mix: line 1 and line 4 are purpose clauses; line 2 and line 5 are result clauses (both flagged by ita); line 3 is a negative purpose clause with nē.

📚 PART 2 · ENTER PETRONIUS 14 min
🏛️ PETRONIUS AND THE CĒNA TRĪMALCHIŌNIS

Gaius Petronius (died 66 CE) was Nero's arbiter elegantiae. His Satyrica is the earliest surviving Latin prose fiction, and its Cēna Trīmalchiōnis is the sharpest satire of new money in all of antiquity. Trimalchio, a freed slave turned millionaire, spends without limit and without taste. Watch how Petronius stacks result clauses (ita…ut, tam…ut) to make the excess feel almost unbelievable.

Trīmalchiō Advenīt

TRĪMALCHIŌ ADVENĪT — ADAPTED FROM PETRONIUS, SATYRICA 27–28
1.Cum ad iānuam convīviī pervēnimus, mīra omnia vīdimus.
2.Trīmalchiō, lībertus dīvitissimus, convīvium ita magnificum parāverat ut nēmō crēderet.
3.Servī per aulam cucurrērunt nē quid deesset.
4.Dominus ipse, balneum ēgressus, intrāvit ut omnibus suam glōriam ostentāret.
5.Tam dīves erat ut nē nōmina quidem omnium servōrum suōrum scīret.
6.Omnēs hospitēs tam attonitī erant ut vix loquī possent.
Gloss: iānuam — gate, entrance (acc.) · dīvitissimus — wealthiest (superlative of dīves) · magnificum — magnificent, lavish · aulam — hall (acc.) · deesset — would be lacking (impf. subj. of dēsum) · balneum ēgressus — having left the bath (perf. participle of ēgredior, deponent) · hospitēs — guests (nom. pl.) · attonitī — astonished · vix — barely, scarcely
✍️ PART 3 · TRANSLATE — THEN CHECK 8 min

Commit a full translation in writing for each, then reveal. Name the clause type as part of your answer.

1. Palaestrio Pleusiclēn hortātus est ut Philocōmasiam quam celerrimē abdūceret.

Palaestrio urged Pleusicles to lead Philocomasium away as quickly as possible. — ut…abdūceret: purpose clause (secondary sequence); hortātus est: deponent perfect of hortor.

2. Trīmalchiō, lībertus dīvitissimus, convīvium ita magnificum parāverat ut nēmō crēderet.

Trimalchio, the wealthiest freedman, had prepared the dinner party so magnificently that no one would believe it. — ita magnificum: intensity signal → result clause; ut nēmō crēderet: imperfect subjunctive (secondary sequence after pluperfect parāverat).

3. Tam dīves erat ut nē nōmina quidem omnium servōrum scīret.

He was so rich that he did not even know the names of all his slaves. — tam…ut: result clause; nē…quidem: “not even” (note: this nē is NOT a purpose nē — it is the idiom nē…quidem).
